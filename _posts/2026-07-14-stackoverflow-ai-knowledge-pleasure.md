# Is AI killing knowledge?
This question is tackled in specific ways in two recent readings, and both invoke a version of this chart:

<img width="3566" height="1742" alt="Monthly Stackoverflow posts (including deleted ones), 2008-2025" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/8091c9c3-aeed-4e11-8048-21155317613c" />

[Stackoverflow](https://stackoverflow.com) is a popular Q&A forum for developers. After a period of growth and popularity, since end of 2022 it suffered [a brutal drop in postings](https://meta.stackoverflow.com/questions/437921/how-does-the-continued-decline-in-posts-since-may-25-influence-our-interpretati) and is by now barely used.
The principal factor for this decrease in popularity seems to be that developers now get their responses by asking their questions to ChatGPT or Claude instead.

Conversations with ChatGPT are private, so whatever is learned there is not versed to the pool of public knowledge and do not benefit the wider community.

A paper by economist and Nobel prize winner [Daron Acemoglu](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2024/acemoglu/facts/) et al. (2026) looks at the consequences of this logic.
Because individuals solve their problems in private conversations and don't share their solutions publicly as they used to, the pool of general knowledge gets slowly exhausted, leading to a "knowledge collapse". The fate of Stackoverflow, explicitly discussed in their introduction, is taken as what's to come generally.

The same graph of the decline of postings on Stackoverflow is discussed by [Sam Learner](https://www.ft.com/sam-learner), graphics journalist and developer at the Financial Times.
In "[After the vibe-code revolution](https://www.ft.com/content/cec8df9e-b43b-4cd1-8feb-c07e804e8d33?syn-25a6b1a6=1)", Learner examines the fate of the developers who volunteer on their free time to keep coding tools functional and evolving (they are called "maintainers", the tools are called "librairies" or "frameworks" or else, depending).

With AI erupting, these maintainers didn't see their task made easier, quite the contrary.
They now receive tons of AI-generated contributions to the code they are in charge of.
These contributions are more often than not unnecessary, unqualified, and of poor quality.
Maintainers have to spend time triaging these instead of investing their time on important issues. As a consequence, they find their job less rewarding and some of them have stopped outright sollicitating [all contributions on critical issues](https://daniel.haxx.se/blog/2026/01/26/the-end-of-the-curl-bug-bounty/).

In this article, the chart showing the virtual disappearing of posts on Stackoverflow is invoked to support the same argument as Acemoglu: the fate of Stackoverflow shows that "There is still human-interaction data being generated, but that's private now" [quote of [Joahnnes Wachs](https://johanneswachs.com/), [academic and author of this paper](https://academic.oup.com/pnasnexus/article/3/9/pgae400/7754871?login=false)].

But besides the threat to public knowledge production, Learner's article explores another consequence of AI, at least as important:

# AI and the diminishing pleasure of making stuff
The point is, there is less fun in working on a project when the anticipated users will be anonymous bots rather than fellow humans.
Now that programming is mostly performed by AI, the worth of the coding tool you are developing is not going to be a matter of human taste and appreciation, nor depending on the web of human relations that you have developed - the communities - across years and decades.
Instead, you now work for machines.
This might be exagerated, after all, software pieces have always had other software pieces as key end users.
But it is a strong and demotivating psychological factor to think that as of now, for real, humans largely rely on AI to build their software: so that decisions regarding which software tools to use are increasingly delegated to AI.

> (incidentally, this opens a vast question about the ergonomy of programming languages. Their raison d'être was to be intelligible to humans. But now, shouldn't they be first intelligible to AI?)
